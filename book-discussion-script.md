Speaker: Distribution Manager, Lava International (Sales and Distribution stint, Sikher district)

Speaking time: about 12–14 minutes at a calm pace (about 1,700 words)

## 1. Opening and Summary (about 1.5 min)

Good [morning/afternoon], everyone. I'm [name], a Distribution Manager at Lava International, currently on my sales and distribution stint in Sikher district. I read chapters 1 to 9 of the *HBR Guide to Generative AI for Managers* by Elisa Farri and Gabriele Rosani of Capgemini.

The one-line summary is that **generative AI is not just a tool for drafting emails. It can be a Co-Thinker, a thought partner that helps managers solve problems and make decisions.**

The book starts with a "knowing-doing gap". A Capgemini survey of 1,100 leaders in 14 countries found that gen AI is on the boardroom agenda in **95%** of organisations. But a separate survey of 1,400 managers found only **15%** use it at least once a day. Leadership believes in it, but managers on the ground aren't using it yet. As a young leader in the field, I felt that gap was personal, and the book's message is that I can't delegate it. I have to try it myself.

The book has five sections. I covered the first two: **the essentials in chapters 1 to 4**, and **managing yourself in chapters 5 to 9**.

## 2. Executive Summary (about 1 min)

In four points:
1. **Machines have moved from tools to assistants to collaborators.** Since ChatGPT launched in November 2022, anyone can talk to a machine in plain language.
2. **There are two modes.** *Co-Pilot* executes a task I direct. *Co-Thinker* is a dialogue partner that challenges my thinking.
3. **The right mindset is conversational, experimental and responsible**, and it helps me avoid five traps.
4. **For managing myself, gen AI helps in four areas.** Productivity and content generation are Co-Pilot tasks. Personal growth and persuasive communication are Co-Thinker tasks.

Now let me go chapter by chapter, with what each means for my job in distribution.

## 3. Chapter-by-Chapter

### Chapter 1: Machines Evolve from Tools into Collaborators (about 1 min)

The book describes three stages. First, **machines as tools**: computers, email, search and smartphones helped us, but strategy and creativity stayed human. Second, **machines as assistants**: Siri in 2011, Cortana and Alexa in 2014 and Google Assistant in 2016 could talk, but Satya Nadella called them "dumb as a rock". Third, **machines as collaborators**: ChatGPT reached 100 million monthly users in two months, compared to about nine months for TikTok and two and a half years for Instagram. The book says "suddenly, everyone was a programmer, even if they did not have any coding skill."

**For my role:** In distribution, I used to think of technology as reporting tools like sales dashboards and stock sheets. The shift is that I can now *talk* to a machine about a problem, for example "why might secondary sales be dropping in this area?", the way I'd talk to a senior colleague. The book also reminds me that any tool I use must follow company data and cybersecurity policy.

### Chapter 2: Co-Pilot and Co-Thinker (about 1.5 min)

This is the core chapter.

**Co-Pilot** is for execution. I direct and validate. Good prompts are **precise and contextual, specify the output format, give examples, and set boundaries**. The warning is that a Co-Pilot is not an autopilot, because gen AI can produce *hallucinations* (confident but wrong answers) and *fabrications* (invented sources).

**Co-Thinker** is a dialogue. The book gives four steps: **assign a role, define the setting, outline the dialogue, create the prompt**. It asks the AI to wait for my feedback at each step.

Three differences: a Co-Pilot *answers*, a Co-Thinker *dialogues*. A Co-Pilot *executes for me*, a Co-Thinker *collaborates with me*. A Co-Pilot helps me *go fast*, a Co-Thinker helps me *pause and reflect*.

**For my role:** Co-Pilot is for tasks like turning a distributor's monthly numbers into a clean summary. Co-Thinker is for decisions like how to handle a distributor who is overstocked on slow-moving models. I would ask the AI to play the distributor, so I can rehearse the conversation before I have it in person.

### Chapter 3: A New Mindset (about 1.5 min)

Gen AI talks with you, it evolves quickly, and it can hallucinate. So the book asks for three mindset traits: **Conversational, Experimental and Responsible**.

It also names **five traps**: the **trust trap** (accepting output because it sounds good), the **fabrication trap** (believing invented facts), the **conformity trap** (accepting generic ideas), the **speed trap** (rushing without reflection), and the **solo trap** (using AI alone instead of with the team). The risks to manage are **privacy, intellectual property, bias and sustainability**.

**For my role:** The risk that matters most to me is privacy. Distributor margins, scheme details, pricing and sales data are commercially sensitive, so I will use only company-approved tools and never paste confidential data into a public model. The solo trap also matters. My value in the field comes from my team and distributors, so I plan to share useful prompts with my sales team and encourage them to experiment. The book says this learning "can't be delegated".

### Chapter 4: 35 Managerial Tasks (about 45 sec)

This chapter is the map for the whole book. It lists **35 tasks in four areas**: managing yourself, your team, your business and change, split between Co-Pilot and Co-Thinker tasks. They are general, so any manager can start today.

**For my role:** I mapped my week against the list. Summarising reports and drafting messages are Co-Pilot tasks. Preparing for distributor negotiations and reflecting on my leadership are Co-Thinker tasks. That map tells me which mode to use and when.

### Chapter 5: Self-Management Tasks (about 45 sec)

This chapter sets up Section Two. For managing myself, **Co-Pilot** handles personal productivity and content generation, where the benefit is speed. **Co-Thinker** handles personal growth and persuasive communication, where the benefits are structure and reflection. Some tasks need both, for example co-thinking a speech and then having the Co-Pilot build the slides.

**For my role:** As a young leader juggling field visits, distributor calls and reporting, I need speed on the routine work so I have time to grow as a leader.

### Chapter 6: Personal Productivity (about 1.5 min)

Three tasks: **email management, time management and summarisation**.
- *Email:* summarise long threads and draft replies, simplified and without jargon. The book's example is Franck, whose emails were full of jargon until he used AI to simplify them and read them from a finance colleague's perspective.
- *Time:* gen AI can summarise my week, prioritise tasks, prepare me for meetings and summarise meetings I missed.
- *Summarisation:* the **Chain of Density** technique asks for a short summary, then repeatedly for a denser one of the same length. The book's example is Juan, who used it on a sales report.

**For my role:** I can use this to summarise long email threads with distributors and my regional team, prepare talking points before a review call, and condense a long sales report into key takeaways. I will ask it to cite where each point came from, and I will check anything involving numbers myself.

### Chapter 7: Content Generation (about 1.5 min)

Two tasks: **text writing and slide creation**.
- *Writing:* the book's flow is context, organise, articulate and validate, then draft. To avoid sounding robotic, I **declare my style, add personal insights, replace exaggerated AI language and seek feedback**.
- *Slides:* gen AI can storyboard, apply templates and add speaking notes. The book's example is Kate, who had one hour after a client call was rescheduled. She gave the AI her notes, goal and audience, got a first draft, and then edited it and added her personal touch.

**For my role:** I can use this for review decks, scheme communication to distributors and retailers, and field-team updates, which I would adapt to local language needs. The caution is the conformity trap. The first draft is a starting point, and my field knowledge of Sikher district has to shape the final message.

### Chapter 8: Personal Growth (about 1.5 min)

This is the first Co-Thinker chapter, with two tasks: **self-reflection on leadership styles and soliciting feedback**.
- *Leadership styles:* research suggests reflection separates good performers from great ones, but most managers don't make it a habit. In the book's dialogue the AI acts as a coach using Daniel Goleman's six leadership styles. It runs a short quiz, I pick a style and a competency, I describe a real struggle, and it suggests actions.
- *Feedback:* people hesitate to give leaders honest feedback. The AI helps me plan how to ask and how to handle my reaction to negative feedback.

The book is clear that AI will not replace human coaches, and that chatbots can give incorrect advice.

**For my role:** As a young leader managing people who may be older or more experienced than me, I could use this to reflect on my style, for example when to be directive and when to coach. I will also use the feedback dialogue before asking my team and distributors how I can support them better. I will treat it as a complement to my mentors.

### Chapter 9: Persuasive Communication (about 1.5 min)

Two tasks: **speech preparation and job interview preparation**.
- *Speech:* a six-step dialogue covering audience, story, supporting data and metaphors, wording, delivery, and likely audience questions. I can even record a rehearsal and ask for feedback. The key warning is that **gen AI tries to please you**, so I should ask for "honest and objective feedback."
- *Interview preparation:* the AI acts as a career coach, matches my résumé to the job description, finds gaps and runs a mock interview.

**For my role:** Persuasion is the heart of distribution. I can use the speech steps before a distributor meet or a review with senior management, and ask the AI to play a tough distributor with objections about margins or credit terms. I can also use the interview dialogue to rehearse for future role discussions.

## 4. Key Learnings and Action Plan (about 1 min)

My four takeaways:
1. **Choose the right mode:** Co-Pilot for speed, Co-Thinker for judgement.
2. **Avoid the five traps** by staying in the loop, verifying facts, adding my own context, slowing down and involving my team.
3. **Protect company and distributor data.**
4. **Experiment and share.**

My 30-day plan:
- **Week 1:** use gen AI daily for email and report summaries, and review every output.
- **Week 2:** run one Co-Thinker dialogue, either the distributor-negotiation rehearsal or the leadership-style reflection.
- **Week 3:** share my best prompt and one lesson with my team.
- **Week 4:** review what saved time, what failed and what to change.

## 5. Closing (about 30 sec)

To close, the authors say this is a chance to be among the first pioneers who "set a pace rather than play catch-up." For a young leader in the field, that is the right ambition. I want to use gen AI to save time on routine work and to think better on the decisions that matter, while keeping human judgement in charge. Thank you. I'd love to hear how others are using it.

## Likely Questions and Short Answers

- **Co-Pilot vs Co-Thinker?** Co-Pilot executes a task I direct and I validate it. Co-Thinker is a dialogue that challenges and builds on my thinking.
- **What if the AI is wrong?** The book names hallucinations and fabrications. I verify facts and numbers against reliable sources, ask for reasoning, and keep the final decision with me.
- **Biggest risk in our business?** Data privacy and the trust trap. I use approved tools only and follow company policy.
- **Won't it make output generic?** That's the conformity trap. I add local context, my voice and my own counterarguments.
- **How will you measure success?** Time saved on routine work, better preparation for key meetings, and how many in my team adopt and share good practices.
