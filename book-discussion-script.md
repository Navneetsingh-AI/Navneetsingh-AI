# Book Discussion Script: HBR Guide to Generative AI for Managers (Chapters 1–9)

Authors: Elisa Farri and Gabriele Rosani (Capgemini), HBR Press, 2025.
Speaking time: about 15–18 minutes at a calm pace.

How to use this:
- Lines in **[BRACKETS]** are places to swap in your own team, tool or number. The evaluators want your role, so please personalise these.
- Facts, figures and framework names come from the book. The "my role" examples are suggestions and are not from the book.
- Where the script says "I tried", only say it if you did. Run one real experiment before the session, because first-hand use is what the book rewards.

---

## 1. Opening and Summary (about 2 min)

Good [morning/afternoon], everyone. I read chapters 1 to 9 of the *HBR Guide to Generative AI for Managers* by Elisa Farri and Gabriele Rosani of Capgemini.

The one-line summary is this: **generative AI is not just a productivity gadget for drafting emails. It can become a Co-Thinker for managers, a thought partner that helps with problem-solving and decision-making.**

The book opens with a "knowing-doing gap". A Capgemini Research Institute survey of 1,100 leaders across 14 countries found gen AI is on the boardroom agenda in **95%** of organisations, and **54%** of executives say their leadership strongly advocates it. But a separate survey of 1,400 managers found that only **15%** use gen AI at least once a day. Leaders believe in it, but managers aren't using it.

The authors' message is that you cannot delegate this. Gen AI needs no coding, so managers need to get their hands dirty, understand the risks, and set an example for their teams.

Chapters 1 to 9 cover two parts:
- **Section One, chapters 1–4: the essentials.** How machines became collaborators, the two modes (Co-Pilot and Co-Thinker), the mindset, and a map of 35 managerial tasks.
- **Section Two, chapters 5–9: managing yourself.** Productivity, content, personal growth and persuasive communication.

## 2. Executive Summary (about 1.5 min)

If I had to give the executive summary in four points:

1. **Machines moved from tools to assistants to collaborators.** The turning point was ChatGPT in November 2022, because anyone can now talk to a machine in plain language.
2. **There are two modes.** In *Co-Pilot* mode AI executes a task and I direct and validate. In *Co-Thinker* mode AI is a sparring partner that challenges my thinking. Use Co-Pilot for speed and Co-Thinker for judgement.
3. **The right mindset is conversational, experimental and responsible.** Without it we fall into five traps: trust, fabrication, conformity, speed and solo.
4. **Applied to myself, gen AI covers four areas.** These are personal productivity, content generation, personal growth, and persuasive communication. The first two are Co-Pilot tasks and the last two are Co-Thinker tasks.

My takeaway is that **the value is not in the tool. It is in how deliberately I direct it and how critically I review what it gives me.**

## 3. Chapter-by-Chapter Discussion

### Chapter 1: Machines Are Evolving from Tools into Collaborators (about 1.5 min)

**What the book says.** There are three paradigms:
- *Machines as tools.* Computers, email, search engines, smartphones and video calls all helped, but creativity and strategy stayed human. No manager thought of a laptop as a collaborator.
- *Machines as assistants.* Siri (2011), Cortana and Alexa (2014) and Google Assistant (2016) could talk, but were limited. Satya Nadella called the first voice assistants "dumb as a rock". The transformer architecture (2017) paved the way for large language models.
- *Machines as collaborators.* ChatGPT reached 100 million monthly users in two months, versus about nine months for TikTok and two and a half years for Instagram. The book says "suddenly, everyone was a programmer, even if they did not have any coding skill."

It also lists the model types: generic public models, specific public models, custom models, and models built into software suites. Whichever one you use must follow your company's legal, ethics, cybersecurity and data policies.

**Why it matters.** Most managerial work will now be a *blend* of human and machine, and that has never happened before.

**My role and practical example.** [In my role as ___], I used to treat AI as a reporting tool. After this chapter I started treating it as a collaborator. For example, instead of only asking it to pull numbers for [a client or team update], I now ask it to challenge how I frame the story.

### Chapter 2: Gen AI as a Co-Pilot and Co-Thinker (about 2.5 min)

This is the central chapter of the book.

**Co-Pilot** is for execution and productivity. I give the direction and validate the output.
Good prompts are **precise and contextual**, **specify the output format**, **give examples** (the "few shots" technique, two to five examples), and **set boundaries**. If I'm stuck, I can ask the AI to write the prompt for me.
The warning: **Co-Pilot is not autopilot.** Gen AI can produce *fabrications* (invented sources) and *hallucinations* (confident but wrong answers), so I stay in the loop.

**Co-Thinker** is for strategic thinking through dialogue. The book gives **four steps to prepare**:
1. **Assign a role** in detail: profile, context and methodology. Examples are a mentor, a devil's advocate or a client.
2. **Define the setting:** one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many or many-to-one.
3. **Outline the dialogue:** a balanced sequence, content and process guardrails, different perspectives, and active participation from me.
4. **Create the prompt.** I can use "chain of thought" by saying "go step by step" and "wait for my feedback before the next step."

**Three differences between the modes:**
- A Co-Pilot *answers*, a Co-Thinker *dialogues*.
- A Co-Pilot *executes for you*, a Co-Thinker *collaborates with you*.
- A Co-Pilot helps you *go fast*, a Co-Thinker helps you *pause and reflect*.

The modes can be chained. I might co-think a problem and then ask the Co-Pilot to write the memo.

**My role and practical example.** Before [an important stakeholder or client meeting], I'd use the book's Q&A example. The AI asks about my topic and audience, generates 10 tough questions, I pick the 3 hardest, it drafts answers, and then it simulates the Q&A so I can practise. [Add your own version, such as pricing pushback or a roadmap review.]

### Chapter 3: A New Mindset That Successful Managers Need to Master (about 2.5 min)

**Three building blocks of the gen AI transformation:** it talks with you, it evolves quickly, and it can hallucinate. Unlike a calculator, it doesn't always give the same answer to the same query. So I need a different mindset from the one I use with error-free technology.

**Five traps to avoid:**
| Trap | What happens | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| Trust | I accept output because it "sounds good" | Ask for reasoning and counterarguments |
| Fabrication | I accept invented facts and sources | Validate against reputable sources |
| Conformity | I take generic ideas as-is | Give company context and push for lateral ideas |
| Speed | I rush without reflecting | Slow down and add my own view |
| Solo | I work only with AI and not my team | Involve colleagues and share learning |

**The mindset in three words: Conversational, Experimental, Responsible.**
- *Conversational:* be clear, patient, focused and polite, and ask the AI for help when stuck.
- *Experimental:* test and share regularly, and try several models. The authors say "you can't delegate this learning."
- *Responsible:* the book lists four risks: **privacy and data protection**, **intellectual property infringement**, **bias and fairness**, and **environmental sustainability**. AI should *augment* human judgement, never replace it.

**My role and practical example.** The risk I take most seriously is data privacy. I only use [approved/enterprise tools] and never paste confidential client or company data into public models. I'm also trying the book's habit tip: a sticky note asking "Have you asked the gen AI model?" For my team, [I'd run a short session where we share one prompt that worked and one that failed, to counter the solo trap].

### Chapter 4: 35 Managerial Tasks That Can Be Enhanced (about 1 min)

This chapter is the map for the rest of the book. It lists **35 tasks across four areas**: managing yourself, your team, your business and change. They are split between Co-Pilot tasks (execution) and Co-Thinker tasks (reflection). The tasks are general, so any manager can start today. The book encourages us to put it down and try a prompt.

**My role and practical example.** I mapped my own week to this table. [Name two or three tasks from your week.] For instance, summarising reports is a Co-Pilot task, while preparing for a negotiation is a Co-Thinker task.

## 4. Section Two: Managing Yourself (chapters 5–9)

### Chapter 5: Self-Management Tasks Enhanced with Gen AI (about 1 min)

This chapter frames the section with a 2×2 split:
- **Co-Pilot:** personal productivity (email, time, summarisation) and content generation (text, slides). The benefit is *speed*.
- **Co-Thinker:** personal growth (leadership styles, feedback) and persuasive communication (speeches, interviews). The benefits are *methodological guidance and structured reflection*.

Some tasks need both modes. The book's example is co-thinking a speech and then using the Co-Pilot to build the slides.

### Chapter 6: Gen AI for Personal Productivity (about 2 min)

**Email management.** Tools like Gemini in Gmail or Copilot in Outlook summarise long threads and draft replies. I can ask the AI to shorten sentences, simplify jargon, make the call to action more direct, and check the message for unconscious bias. *Case in the book:* Franck, a middle manager, got feedback that his emails were jargon-heavy. He used gen AI to analyse his drafts, simplify the language, and read them from a finance colleague's perspective.

**Time management.** Gen AI can summarise my week, prioritise tasks, pull information for upcoming meetings, and summarise meetings I missed, including any action items for me.

**Summarisation.** The book's *Chain of Density* technique starts with a four-sentence summary and then repeatedly asks for a denser summary of the same length. Juan, the middle manager in the book's example, used it on a Q1 sales report. I should specify length, depth and accuracy, and check the output for tone, misinterpretation and bias.

**My role and practical example.** [Example: I now ask the AI to summarise long email threads and prep me before client calls, and to turn a 30-page report into takeaways with page references.] The time saved goes to [analysis or team coaching]. I still read anything sensitive myself.

### Chapter 7: Gen AI for Content Generation (about 2 min)

**Text writing.** The book's flow runs *Context, Organise, Articulate, Validate*, then draft. Gen AI helps me beat the blank page, set the tone, tailor content to an audience, and edit. To avoid sounding robotic, I should **declare my style, add personal insights, replace exaggerated AI language, and seek feedback**. The warning is "Write smarter, with oversight," because the trust trap applies to documents too.

**Slide creation.** Gen AI can storyboard, apply templates, add visuals and write speaking notes. *Case in the book:* Kate, an innovation director at a biotech startup, had one hour after a client rescheduled. She gave the AI her notes, goal, audience and key messages, and got a first draft on the company template. She then edited it and added her personal touch. The caution is conformity: slides must still stand out and carry my own voice.

**My role and practical example.** [Example: I use AI for a first-draft structure of my decks and proposals, but I rewrite the opening and the key message in my own voice.] I treat the AI draft as 60–70% of the work, and the judgement and storytelling stay mine.

### Chapter 8: Gen AI for Personal Growth (about 2 min)

This is the first of the Co-Thinker chapters.

**Self-reflection on leadership styles.** Research suggests reflection separates good performers from great ones, yet most managers don't make it a habit. In the book's dialogue, the AI acts as an expert coach using Daniel Goleman's six leadership styles. It runs a short quiz, I choose one style to work on, it identifies the emotional-intelligence competency behind it, I give a real example where I struggled, and it suggests actions to start or keep doing. The book notes that a machine can reduce the political fear that blocks candid feedback, but it will not replace human coaches. It describes coaching chatbots such as Marshall Goldsmith's, with a caution that they can give incorrect advice.

**Soliciting feedback.** People are often reluctant to give feedback to leaders. The dialogue has five steps: describe my current approach, get improvement methods, pick one, plan the actions, then learn the pitfalls and how to handle emotional reactions to negative feedback.

**My role and practical example.** [Example: I ran the leadership-style dialogue and found I default to ___. I picked ___ and set one action for the next month. I'll also use the feedback dialogue before my next review cycle.] I'd suggest the same to my team members as a private, low-risk starting point.

### Chapter 9: Gen AI for Persuasive Communication (about 2 min)

**Speech preparation.** The six-step dialogue is: (1) share topic and audience, (2) frame the story (beginning, middle, end), (3) add data, quotes, analogies and metaphors, (4) develop wording and tone, (5) get advice on delivery such as rhythm, pauses, gestures and posture, (6) rehearse with three likely audience questions. I can also upload a rehearsal recording for feedback. The key warning is that **gen AI tries to please you**, so I should ask for "honest and objective feedback" and "constructive criticism, not validation."

**Job interview preparation.** The AI acts as a career coach. The steps are to share the job description, match it with my résumé and find gaps, adjust the résumé, run a mock interview that increases in difficulty, and then get feedback. The same method works for promotion conversations and stakeholder pitches.

**My role and practical example.** [Example: Before my next client or leadership presentation, I'll use the six steps, record a rehearsal, and ask for honest critique. I'll also use the interview dialogue to rehearse a promotion or appraisal conversation.]

## 5. Key Learnings and Action Plan (about 1.5 min)

My top takeaways from chapters 1–9:
1. **Use the right mode.** Co-Pilot for speed on routine tasks, Co-Thinker for reflection on judgement tasks.
2. **Never fall into the traps.** Stay in the loop, verify sources, add context and slow down.
3. **Protect data.** Use approved tools and never paste confidential information into public models.
4. **Experiment and share.** The learning can't be delegated, and sharing avoids the solo trap.

My commitments for the next 30 days:
- **Week 1:** Use gen AI daily for email and meeting summaries, and review every output.
- **Week 2:** Run one Co-Thinker dialogue, either the leadership-style reflection or the speech preparation.
- **Week 3:** Share my best prompt and one lesson learned with my team.
- **Week 4:** Review what saved time, what failed, and what I should stop doing.

## 6. Closing (about 30 sec)

To close, the book's central idea is that gen AI is a collaborator, and a collaborator is only as good as the direction and critical judgement we bring. The authors say we can be among the first pioneers to "set a pace rather than play catch-up." I intend to do that, and I'd like to hear how others here are using it. Thank you.

## Likely Questions and Short Answers

- **What is the difference between Co-Pilot and Co-Thinker?** Co-Pilot executes a task I direct and I validate it. Co-Thinker is a dialogue where AI challenges and builds on my thinking, so it is for reflection rather than speed.
- **What if the AI is wrong?** The book names hallucinations and fabrications. I verify facts against reputable sources, ask the AI for its reasoning, and keep human judgement as the final step.
- **What is the biggest risk for us?** Data privacy, plus the trust trap. I use only approved tools and follow company policy.
- **Won't AI make us lazy or generic?** That is the conformity and speed trap. The antidote is to add company context, my own voice and my own counterarguments.
- **How will you measure success?** Time saved on routine tasks, quality of preparation for key meetings, and how many in my team adopt and share good practices. [Add your own metric.]
